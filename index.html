About

I'm a GIS specialist with a post-graduate diploma from Fleming College, and my training there was hands-on from the start: spatial analysis, remote sensing, web GIS, and automation with Python and FME. The coursework ranged widely, from LiDAR processing and object-based image analysis to network analysis and regression modelling, and I built interactive web applications along the way. For my capstone, I mapped potential tree planting sites across Peterborough's five wards. The work supported the city's long-term urban forestry goals, and it combined LiDAR-derived canopy analysis, land surface temperature mapping, and multi-layer spatial conflict modelling. Beyond coursework, the projects I enjoy most are the ones where spatial data reveals a pattern that is not obvious at first glance. I like layering methods - statistical, network-based, and visual - to build a fuller picture of what the data is actually saying, and what it means for the people and places involved. I'm looking for work in urban planning, environmental management, or municipal government - places where GIS feeds into real decisions, and where I can keep learning by applying new spatial methods to real problems.

Kitchener Tree Canopy & Park Access - Personal Project

Spatial Analysis · Python / GeoPandas · City of Kitchener Open Data

Tree canopy cover by ward - Ward 5, outlined in red, ranks first for new planting. Click to enlarge.

This is a personal project I built in my own time, with free and open-source tools only. I wanted to answer a practical question: which parts of Kitchener have the least tree canopy and the weakest access to parks, and where would new tree planting do the most good?

I worked from the City of Kitchener's open data - ward boundaries, the park and trail inventories, the city tree inventory of 99,493 trees, and census canopy estimates. Each of the 10 wards was measured the same way and then ranked. Ward 5 came out as the clear priority: it has the lowest canopy in the city at 17.2%, and the least park access, with only 69.1% of its area within 800 m of a park.

ARCPY AUTOMATION
Raster Band Analyser -NDVI

This script automates NDVI calculation from multispectral imagery using ArcPy Spatial Analyst, and outputs classified rasters for vegetation analysis workflows, with no manual band selection needed.

import arcpy
from arcpy.sa import *

# Calculate NDVI from multispectral bands
nir = Float(Raster("band_4"))
red = Float(Raster("band_3"))
ndvi = (nir - red) / (nir + red)

ndvi.save("output/ndvi_classified.tif")
print(f"Range: {ndvi.minimum:.3f} to {ndvi.maximum:.3f}")

An end-to-end ArcPy pipeline for Ottawa crime data: it imports CSV coordinates into a geodatabase feature class, reprojects to UTM Zone 18N, runs Optimized Hot Spot Analysis (the Gi* statistic), reads the results with a SearchCursor, and exports a ranked CSV report.

# 1. Create point feature class from CSV
sr = arcpy.SpatialReference(4326)
arcpy.management.CreateFeatureclass(OUTPUT_GDB, "Ottawa_Crime_Raw", "POINT", spatial_reference=sr)
with arcpy.da.InsertCursor(out_fc, ["SHAPE@XY", "Neighbourhood", "Crime_Rate"]) as cur:
    for row in neighbourhoods:
        cur.insertRow([(float(row["Longitude"]), float(row["Latitude"])),
                       row["Neighbourhood"], float(row["Crime_Rate_per1000"])])

# 2. Reproject to NAD 1983 UTM Zone 18N
arcpy.management.Project(in_fc, out_fc, arcpy.SpatialReference(26918))

# 3. Optimized Hot Spot Analysis (Gi* statistic)
arcpy.stats.OptimizedHotSpotAnalysis(
    Input_Features = os.path.join(OUTPUT_GDB, "Ottawa_Crime_UTM"),
    Output_Features = os.path.join(OUTPUT_GDB, "Ottawa_HotSpots"),
    Analysis_Field  = "Crime_Rate"
)
